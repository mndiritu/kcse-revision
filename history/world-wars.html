WORLD WARS (FORM 3) — WRITING NOTES (KCSE STYLE)

1) Overview: World Wars & why they mattered
  • In the first half of the 20th century, two major wars were fought almost worldwide: the 1st and 2nd World Wars.
  • They were sparked and fuelled by political, military and economic developments in Europe from the late 19th century to the first half of the 20th century.
  • They caused massive loss of life, and a shift of power from Western Europe to North America and Russia.
  • They led to a period of suspicion and rivalry between Western and Eastern blocs, known as the Cold War.
  • Global events inspired nationalism in Africa and contributed to liberation from colonialism.

How to score in KCSE “Explain/Describe” questions

  • State the heading (e.g., “Arms race”), then add a short support line showing how it increased tension/led to war.
  • For Describe the course, use a timeline/sequence: early trigger → alliances activate → fronts → new methods → turning points → end.
  • Keep your points short, clear, and linked (don’t write long stories).
2) WORLD WAR I (1914–1918): introduction
  • WWI began in August 1914.
  • Before the war, Europe dominated world power; major powers included Britain, France, Russia and Germany.
  • It began as a local conflict between Austria and Serbia but escalated into a wider war.
  • It followed a long period of tensions and rivalries, stretching back to the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871) in which France was defeated.
3) Causes of World War I (Long-term + Immediate)

A) Long-term causes

  1. System of alliances (Triple Alliance and Triple Entente)
    • Alliances increased tension and meant a local conflict could quickly involve many countries.
    • Key developments included: Dual Alliance (Germany + Austria-Hungary, 1879), Triple Alliance (adding Italy, 1882), Franco-Russian Alliance (1892), Dual Entente (Britain + France, 1904), and Russia joining to form the Triple Entente.
  2. Arms race
    • European powers built stronger armies and navies; competition created suspicion and readiness to fight.
  3. Economic rivalry
    • Competition for raw materials and markets, especially between Germany and Britain, raised hostility.
  4. Colonial rivalry
    • Competition for colonies (raw materials + prestige) created mistrust; France also wanted revenge after losing Alsace-Lorraine.
  5. Nationalism
    • France wanted Alsace-Lorraine back; in Eastern Europe, Balkan nationalities disliked Austria-Hungary rule and wanted independence.
  6. Italo-Turkish dispute (1911)
    • Italy attacked Turkey and annexed Tripoli (Libya), increasing tension and rivalries.
  7. Moroccan crises (1905–06 and 1911)
    • Germany opposed France’s position in Morocco; crises increased bitterness and tension between powers.
  8. Balkan wars (1912–1913)
    • Turkey’s weakness, Balkan conflicts, and Austria-Hungary vs Serbia rivalry created a dangerous situation that set the stage for war in 1914.

B) Immediate cause

  • Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (heir to Austria) and his wife Sophie on 28 June 1914 in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip.
  • He was backed by a secret Serb organization called “Union” or “Death” (the Black Hand).
  • The assassination triggered the alliances already in place.

Austria’s ultimatum to Serbia (23 July 1914): key demands

  1. Explanation for the assassination.
  2. Apologies to Austria-Hungary.
  3. Suppression of anti-Austrian publications/organizations.
  4. Austrian officials to participate in inquiry and judicial proceedings.
  5. Dismissal of officials Austria objected to.
  6. Austrian police allowed into Serbia to ensure demands were fulfilled.
4) Course of World War I (Describe)

Fast timeline (high-yield)

  • 28 June 1914: Assassination in Sarajevo.
  • 23 July 1914: Austria issues ultimatum to Serbia.
  • 28 July 1914: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
  • 30 July 1914: Russia orders full mobilization.
  • 1 Aug 1914: Germany declares war on Russia.
  • 3 Aug 1914: Germany declares war on France.
  • 4 Aug 1914: Germany invades neutral Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany.
  • 11 Nov 1918 (11:00am): Guns fall silent (end of WWI).

A) The main camps

  • Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Turkey and Italy (as listed in the notes for the early period).
  • Allies: Serbia, Montenegro, Belgium, France, Russia and Great Britain.
  • The war spread beyond Europe and was fought in Europe, Africa and Asia.

B) Fronts / areas of fighting

  • In Europe: Western Front and Eastern Front.
  • Western Front: Germans fought British and French (later American) forces; trench warfare developed after Germans pulled back across the Marne River.
  • Eastern Front: Russia fought Germany and Austria-Hungary; Russia had heavy casualties and later withdrew after the 1917 revolution.
  • Sea warfare also occurred; Britain’s naval power enabled a blockade that hurt Central Powers.

C) War methods/technology mentioned

  • Trench warfare (digging trenches).
  • Use of poisonous gas (Germans).
  • Use of tanks (British).

D) End of WWI (key points)

  • 1917 events shaped the end: Russia withdrew after the 1917 revolution (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk), while USA entered the war.
  • Germany’s situation worsened; troops mutinied; Kaiser fled; Germany became a republic.
  • Germany signed an armistice after being given stiff terms; guns went silent on 11 Nov 1918.
5) Why USA joined the war (as given in the notes)
  • Germany’s unrestricted submarine warfare affected neutral states; (example given: sinking of the Sussex carrying American passengers).
  • Zimmerman Telegram: German foreign minister urged Mexico to attack USA if USA joined the war; leaked and published.
  • Pressure from Americans with relatives/friends in Britain/France.
  • USA feared Allied defeat would harm USA industrial/financial interests (loans and contracts).
  • Intelligence linked German sympathizers to industrial sabotage in USA.
6) Factors that led to defeat of Central Powers (WWI)
  • Allies had more manpower (e.g., Britain, France, USA, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa).
  • Allies had more financial and industrial resources.
  • Allies had more powerful weapons (tanks, aircraft, battleships).
  • Allies had strong political leadership (e.g., Lloyd George; Georges Clemenceau).
  • Entry of the USA gave Allies advantage.
  • Germany was weakened by allies’ problems/defections (Italy decamped; others needed support; Turkey accepted defeat).
  • Germany’s invasion of neutral Belgium turned world opinion against Central Powers.
  • Unity among Allies and leadership (General Foch).
  • British naval blockade caused severe food shortages for Central Powers.
7) Peace settlement after WWI (Treaty of Versailles & others)
  • Peace conference held in January 1919.
  • USA President Woodrow Wilson outlined 14 principles as a basis for peace (see below).
  • Treaties signed with the five Central Powers, including:
    • Versailles (Germany) — 28 June 1919
    • St. German (Austria) — 10 Sept 1919
    • Neuilly (Bulgaria) — 27 Nov 1919
    • Trianon (Hungary) — 4 June 1920
    • Sevres (Turkey) — Aug 1920 (later renamed Lausanne, 1923)

Wilson’s “14 Principles” (as listed)

  1. Abolition of secret diplomacy
  2. Free navigation of sea (war & peace)
  3. Removal of economic barriers between states
  4. Reduction of armament
  5. Vacation of Russian territory by German forces
  6. Adjustment of colonial claims in interests of people concerned
  7. Restore independence to Belgium
  8. Restore freedom to France; return Alsace & Lorraine
  9. Independence to Poland; access to sea
  10. Self-determination for Austria-Hungary
  11. Adjust Italian frontiers based on nationality
  12. Self-government for non-Turkish people in Turkish empire; open Dardanelles
  13. Evacuate Serbia, Montenegro, Romania; Serbia access to sea
  14. Create association of world nations to preserve peace

Results of Treaty of Versailles (key points from notes)

  • Germany lost colonial possessions (under League of Nations supervision): Rwanda-Urundi, Tanganyika, Namibia, Cameroon, Togo.
  • Germany to pay war reparations (over 6.5 billion Sterling Pounds).
  • Germany disarmed; limited forces; air force banned.
  • Alsace-Lorraine returned to France.
  • Poland regained territory and Danzig declared a free state.
  • Rhineland permanently demilitarized.
  • Saar administered by League for 15 years; then vote to decide whether to belong to France or Germany.
  • Treaty blamed Germany for the outbreak of the war; Germany punished heavily.

Why the peace settlement largely failed (as explained)

  • Germany felt humiliated and unfairly blamed/punished.
  • Interests of colonial peoples were ignored; former colonies were transferred to France/Britain.
  • Minorities’ issues inadequately handled (e.g., Austria forbidden to merge with Germany).
  • Italy felt unfairly treated and walked out of conference.
  • USA did not join League of Nations due to constitutional/foreign policy reasons.
8) Results/Effects of World War I (as in notes)
  • Over 13 million people died; increased widows and orphans.
  • Spread of diseases among soldiers; influenza outbreak after the war.
  • Mass starvation as many farmers went to war leaving land/crops unattended.
  • Massive destruction of property (cities, villages, schools, factories, railways, roads, bridges).
  • Huge spending on weapons and soldiers’ recruitment, feeding, treatment, transport.
  • USA emerged as a leading power; economy boosted by supplying goods/food.
  • Europe’s boundaries altered; some territories gained independence (examples given include Poland, Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia; and others).
  • Decline of European overseas dominance; Africans/Indians returned with revolutionary ideas.
  • Displacement and refugees; misery and suffering.
  • Some positive developments: international organization idea (League of Nations), advances in surgery/pharmaceuticals, wider use of motor vehicles/aircraft, women working more (gender equality gains).
9) League of Nations (origin, aims, structure, success & failure)

A) Origin

  • Idea pushed by Woodrow Wilson.
  • Constitution adopted by Paris Peace Conference in April 1919.
  • Headquarters: Geneva, Switzerland (neutral country).
  • Main objective: preserve international peace and security.

B) Aims (as stated)

  • Maintain international peace and security.
  • Encourage peaceful settlement of disputes and arbitration.
  • Take action against aggressors (sanctions or military action).
  • Improve living and working conditions (seen as supporting peace).
  • Supervise territories taken from Central Powers (mandates).

C) Main organs

  • Assembly
  • Council
  • Secretariat
  • Permanent Court of International Justice
  • International Labour Organization / Office

D) High-yield functions (KCSE favourites)

  • Assembly: admit new members; control budget; supervise Council; appoint judges; approve Secretary-General; approve treaties; amend covenant.
  • Council: implement Assembly recommendations; prepare agenda; deal with global peace issues; appoint/control committees.
  • Secretariat: prepare agenda; correspondence; publish reports; research; implement decisions; register treaties; continuity between meetings.
  • Permanent Court: settle disputes; advise Council/Assembly (based at The Hague).
  • International Labour Office: improve workers’ welfare and working conditions.

E) Achievements (as listed)

  • Encouraged peaceful settlement through International Court of Justice.
  • Maintained peace/security in Danzig despite hostility between Poles and Germans.
  • Held disarmament meetings; regulated manufacture/sale of arms (some control).
  • Supervised mandated territories and international control areas (e.g., Danzig City, Saar Valley).
  • Settled some inter-state disputes (examples given: Colombia vs Peru; Poland vs Germany over Northern Silesia).
  • Helped refugees; promoted humane treatment of minorities and war victims.
  • Promoted socio-economic cooperation (labour improvements; financial stability in Austria).
  • Laid groundwork for formation of the United Nations (1945).

F) Failures (examples from notes)

  • Failed to stop Germany from violating the Paris peace terms (re-militarization, navy, airbase).
  • Failed to stop aggression: Japan in Manchuria (1931), Italy in Ethiopia (1935), Germany in Rhineland, Russia in Finland (1939).
  • Disarmament commission failed to persuade states to reduce armaments.
  • States continued making defensive pacts, disregarding Versailles.
  • Insufficient funds; undermined by Conference of Ambassadors in Paris.

G) Reasons why League failed (as argued)

  • Seen as linked to Versailles and dominated by Allied powers (appeared biased).
  • Lacked enough funds.
  • USA rejected membership, weakening it financially and psychologically.
  • Weak covenant made decisive action hard against aggressors.
  • Absence/delayed membership of major powers (Germany joined late; USSR joined later).
  • Members prioritized sovereignty/self-interest over collective action.
  • Conference of Ambassadors undermined the League’s authority.
