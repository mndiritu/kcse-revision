Genetic Terms (Form 4)

Topic Tools

Tip: Genetics becomes easy when you can define the terms cleanly and then use them in a Punnett square. KCSE rewards clarity, not vibes.

GENETIC TERMS — WRITING NOTES (KCSE)

1) Big picture: what you must be able to do

Genetics questions in KCSE mainly test whether you can:

  • Define key terms accurately (gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, etc.).
  • Use symbols correctly (T, t; XH, Xh; IA, IB, i).
  • Explain inheritance using Mendel’s ideas (dominance, segregation, independent assortment).
  • Draw and interpret Punnett squares for monohybrid, dihybrid and sex-linked crosses.

KCSE exam language (use these exact phrases)

  • Gene is a section of DNA that controls a characteristic.
  • Alleles are alternative forms of the same gene found at the same locus.
  • Genotype is the genetic makeup; phenotype is the observable expression.
  • Meiosis produces haploid gametes, helping maintain chromosome number after fertilisation.
2) Genetic structures (the “hardware”)

These are the physical structures that carry hereditary information.

Key terms + KCSE definitions

  • Nucleus: part of the cell containing chromosomes (genetic material).
  • Chromosome: thread-like structures in the nucleus carrying genes.
  • Chromatid: one of two identical halves of a replicated chromosome.
  • Centromere: region joining sister chromatids.
  • Gene: a length of DNA controlling a characteristic.
  • Locus: position of a gene on a chromosome.
  • DNA: hereditary material that makes up genes.

Simple diagram: chromosome, chromatids and centromere

two chromatids joined at a centromere.

Small but deadly KCSE trap

A chromosome carries many genes. A gene is a segment of DNA. Don’t mix these like people mix “county” and “ward”.

3) Genetic information & expression (genotype → phenotype)

KCSE often tests whether you can separate the “code” from the “appearance”.

Core terms you must define cleanly

  • Trait/character: a feature (e.g. height, blood group).
  • Characteristic: a specific form of a trait (e.g. tall, short).
  • Allele: alternative form of the same gene (e.g. T and t).
  • Genotype: the genetic constitution (e.g. TT, Tt, tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable appearance (e.g. tall or short).
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt).
  • Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).

Memory muscle (write these as your “anchor lines”)

  • Genotype = what you have (letters).
  • Phenotype = what you see (appearance).
4) Dominance relationships (exam gold)

Dominance describes how alleles interact to produce the phenotype in a heterozygote. KCSE mainly tests three relationships: complete dominance, incomplete dominance, and codominance. The difference becomes easy once you focus on one thing: What does the heterozygote look like?

Core terms (KCSE definitions)

  • Dominant allele: an allele expressed in a heterozygote.
  • Recessive allele: an allele expressed only when homozygous.
  • Dominance: interaction of alleles where one may mask, form an intermediate, or appear alongside another.

Exam tip: Define dominance using the heterozygous state. That’s where KCSE checks understanding.

The key question KCSE is asking

If an organism is heterozygous (e.g. Tt), does it look like: the dominant, an intermediate, or both at once?

A clean comparison (learn this like a chant)

Type Heterozygote looks like… Common KCSE clue Typical phenotype ratio (F2)
Complete dominance Same as the dominant homozygote One allele “masks” the other 3 : 1
Incomplete dominance Intermediate (a “blend” appearance) Heterozygote has its own distinct phenotype 1 : 2 : 1
Codominance Both traits appear together Two alleles expressed equally (no blending) 1 : 2 : 1 (often in genotype/phenotype)

KCSE pattern: Complete dominance usually gives 3:1 phenotypes in F2. Incomplete dominance and codominance often give 1:2:1 phenotypes.

A) Complete dominance (masking)

In complete dominance, the heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype. The recessive allele is present but not expressed in the heterozygote.

How it looks (simple view)

TT  → Dominant phenotype
Tt  → Dominant phenotype   ✅ (same appearance as TT)
tt  → Recessive phenotype

KCSE marking phrase you can use

“In complete dominance, the dominant allele is expressed in both homozygous and heterozygous states, while the recessive allele is expressed only in the homozygous state.”

B) Incomplete dominance (intermediate)

In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has a phenotype that is intermediate between the two homozygotes. KCSE may loosely call this “blending”, but be careful: the alleles do not vanish — it is the phenotype that appears intermediate.

How it looks (simple view)

RR  → One extreme phenotype (e.g. Red)
Rr  → Intermediate phenotype (e.g. Pink) ✅
rr  → Other extreme phenotype (e.g. White)

In F2, phenotype ratio is usually 1 : 2 : 1 (not 3:1).

Common KCSE trap

Students write 3:1 out of habit. If the question clearly says the heterozygote is intermediate, your ratio should be 1:2:1.

C) Codominance (both expressed)

In codominance, both alleles in a heterozygote are expressed equally. There is no masking and no blending. You see both traits in the phenotype.

KCSE safest example: ABO blood group

  • IA produces antigen A
  • IB produces antigen B
  • In genotype IAIB, both antigens appear → phenotype is blood group AB
  • i is recessive (no A or B antigen)

This is the safest KCSE example for codominance. Use it unless the question gives a different one.

How it looks (simple view)

IᴬIᴬ → Group A
Iᴬi  → Group A
IᴮIᴮ → Group B
Iᴮi  → Group B
IᴬIᴮ → Group AB ✅ (both expressed)
ii   → Group O
Quick check (self-test in 30 seconds)
  1. If the heterozygote looks like one parent only → complete dominance.
  2. If the heterozygote is halfway/in-between → incomplete dominance.
  3. If the heterozygote shows both traits clearly → codominance.
Common KCSE symbols you must handle
  • ABO blood group: IA, IB (codominant), i (recessive).
  • Sex-linked: XHXh, XhY etc. (allele on X chromosome).

KCSE marking is strict on symbols. Write them neatly and consistently.

5) Cell division terms (mitosis vs meiosis)

Genetics is built on how cells divide and how gametes are formed.

  • Mitosis: produces genetically identical diploid cells for growth and repair.
  • Meiosis: produces haploid gametes and creates variation.
  • Gamete: sex cell (sperm/ovum) (haploid).
  • Diploid (2n): two sets of chromosomes (body cells).
  • Haploid (n): one set of chromosomes (gametes).
  • Homologous chromosomes: pair of chromosomes with genes at the same loci.
  • Crossing over: exchange of genetic material between homologous chromosomes (meiosis I).
  • Independent assortment: random distribution of chromosomes into gametes.

6) Inheritance patterns & problem-solving terms

These are the terms used in crosses and genetic calculations.

  • Monohybrid inheritance: inheritance of one pair of contrasting characteristics.
  • Dihybrid inheritance: inheritance of two pairs of contrasting characteristics.
  • F1 generation: first filial generation (offspring from parental cross).
  • F2 generation: offspring from crossing F1 individuals.
  • Punnett square: diagram used to predict genotypes and phenotypes of offspring.
  • Test cross: crossing with a homozygous recessive to determine unknown genotype.
  • Ratio: relationship between numbers (e.g. 3:1; 1:2:1).
  • Probability: chance of an outcome (expressed as fraction or percentage).
Punnett square (monohybrid) demo you can copy in exams

Example: Tall (T) is dominant over short (t). Cross Tt × Tt.

1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt | Phenotype ratio: 3 tall : 1 short

Marking-tip (how to avoid silly losses)

  • Write the gametes first (from each parent).
  • Fill the square systematically.
  • State both genotype ratio and phenotype ratio.
7) Sex determination & sex-linked terms
  • Autosome: any chromosome that is not a sex chromosome.
  • Sex chromosome: X or Y chromosome.
  • Sex-linked trait: trait controlled by a gene on the X chromosome.
  • Carrier: individual who has the allele but does not show the trait (commonly heterozygous female).

KCSE truth (say it plainly)

Many X-linked recessive traits appear more in males because males have only one X chromosome.

8) Variation & mutation terms
  • Variation: differences among individuals of the same species.
  • Continuous variation: no clear categories (e.g. height, mass).
  • Discontinuous variation: distinct categories (e.g. blood group).
  • Mutation: sudden change in genetic material.
  • Gene mutation: change in DNA base sequence within a gene.
  • Chromosomal mutation: change in chromosome structure or number.

Classroom reality (no fancy lab needed)

  • Use class data for continuous variation (e.g. heights): draw a simple frequency table.
  • Use blood groups or tongue rolling for discontinuous examples (where appropriate).
  • For mutation, focus on definition + effect (don’t overcomplicate).
9) How to revise genetic terms without suffering

10-minute “memory muscle” routine

  1. Write 5 anchor definitions: gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, meiosis.
  2. Do one monohybrid Punnett square neatly.
  3. List 3 sources of variation: crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation.
  4. Answer one KCSE-style short question from the drill section.

Consistency beats “one big weekend”. Genetics is like maths: daily small reps.

GENETIC TERMS — QUICK REVISION

Must-know terms (fast list)

  • Gene, DNA, chromosome, locus
  • Allele, dominant, recessive
  • Genotype, phenotype
  • Homozygous, heterozygous
  • Codominance, incomplete dominance, multiple alleles
  • Gamete, diploid (2n), haploid (n), homologous chromosomes
  • Crossing over, independent assortment
  • Punnett square, monohybrid, dihybrid, test cross
  • Autosome, sex chromosome, sex-linked, carrier
  • Variation (continuous/discontinuous), mutation

1) One-line definitions (KCSE style)

  • Gene: a section of DNA controlling a characteristic.
  • Allele: an alternative form of the same gene at the same locus.
  • Genotype: genetic constitution of an organism.
  • Phenotype: observable characteristics of an organism.
  • Meiosis: cell division producing haploid gametes and causing variation.

2) KCSE traps (avoid easy mark losses)

  • Don’t confuse gene with chromosome.
  • Don’t confuse genotype with phenotype.
  • Always state genotype ratio and phenotype ratio separately.
  • In sex-linked questions, remember: males are XY (one X only).
  • Write ABO symbols properly: IA, IB, i (not IA as plain letters).

3) Memory prompts (short, sharp, exam-ready)

  1. Differentiate between a gene and an allele.
  2. Differentiate between genotype and phenotype (with an example).
  3. Define homozygous and heterozygous (with examples).
  4. Explain why meiosis leads to variation (give 2 reasons).
  5. State what a test cross is used for.
  6. State what codominance means using ABO blood group.
